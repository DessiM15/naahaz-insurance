Medicare has more moving parts than it needs to, and most of the expensive
mistakes happen in the first year — often before anyone realises a decision was
even being made. Here are the nine we see most often.

## 1. Missing your Initial Enrollment Period

Your window is seven months wide: the three months before your 65th birthday
month, that month, and the three months after. Miss it without qualifying
coverage elsewhere and the Part B late-enrollment penalty is permanent — it
adds to your premium for as long as you have Medicare.

## 2. Assuming you must enroll at 65 while still working

If you're covered by an employer group plan and the employer has 20 or more
employees, you can usually delay Part B without penalty. If the employer is
smaller, Medicare typically becomes primary and delaying can leave you exposed.
The rule turns on headcount, which is not obvious and worth confirming rather
than assuming.

## 3. Choosing a plan on premium alone

The lowest premium regularly costs the most over a year. What matters is total
expected cost: premium, deductible, copays, and whether your prescriptions sit
on the plan's formulary. A zero-premium plan with your medication in a high tier
can be considerably more expensive than a plan that costs something up front.

## 4. Not checking whether your doctors are in network

Medicare Advantage plans operate networks. Your long-standing specialist may or
may not be in the one you pick, and finding out in February is a bad time to
learn it. Check every provider you actually use before enrolling, not just your
primary.

## 5. Assuming Medicare covers long-term care

It does not, in any meaningful sense. Medicare covers limited skilled nursing
after a qualifying hospital stay. It does not cover custodial care — help with
bathing, dressing, eating, moving around — which is what most people eventually
need. That care is paid out of pocket until savings are gone. This is the single
most expensive misunderstanding in retirement planning.

## 6. Skipping Part D because you don't take medication

Part D has its own late-enrollment penalty, and it is also permanent. Health
changes. A low-cost drug plan taken now is cheaper than the penalty applied to a
plan you'll need in five years.

## 7. Never reviewing the plan again

Plans change their formularies, networks and costs every single year — and so do
your prescriptions. A plan that was right in 2022 may be wrong now. Open
Enrollment each autumn exists precisely for this, and most people let it pass.

## 8. Missing the Medigap open enrollment window

For six months after enrolling in Part B at 65, you can buy any Medicare
Supplement policy regardless of your health. After that, in most states, insurers
can underwrite — and decline. It's a one-time window and it closes quietly.

## 9. Going it alone when help is free

An independent agent is compensated by the carriers, so the premium is the same
whether you enroll yourself or with help. The difference is that someone checks
your prescriptions against the formularies and your doctors against the networks
before you commit to anything.
