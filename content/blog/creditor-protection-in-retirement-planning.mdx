Most retirement planning focuses on accumulation: how much, how fast, in what.
Far less attention goes to a quieter question — if something went wrong, how
much of it could actually be taken?

For business owners, physicians, landlords and anyone else carrying real
liability exposure, that question deserves a proper answer.

## Not all retirement assets are protected equally

This surprises people. Two accounts holding identical investments can have very
different exposure to creditors, depending entirely on the wrapper around them.

**Employer plans** — 401(k)s and most other ERISA-governed plans — generally
carry strong federal protection from creditors.

**IRAs** are protected differently. Federal bankruptcy law shields a substantial
but capped amount of contributory IRA assets, while rollover balances from
employer plans often receive broader treatment. Outside bankruptcy, protection
depends heavily on state law.

**Taxable brokerage accounts** generally have no special protection at all.

**Life insurance and annuities** receive meaningful protection in many states —
Illinois among them — which is one of several reasons they appear in plans where
liability is a genuine concern.

Because so much of this turns on state law and on the specifics of the claim,
none of it is a substitute for advice from an attorney who practices in it. What
it does mean is that the *structure* of your retirement savings, not just the
balance, is worth deliberate thought.

## Where this actually matters

Not everyone needs to think hard about this. It becomes relevant when:

- You own a business and have personal exposure to its liabilities
- You're in a profession that carries meaningful malpractice risk
- You own rental property
- You're a guarantor on business debt
- Your net worth sits well above your liability coverage

## Ordinary steps that help

Before anything exotic, the basics do most of the work:

**Fund employer plans first** where the protection is strongest and the tax
treatment is good anyway.

**Keep rollover money separate.** Commingling a plan rollover with contributory
IRA funds can muddy the protection that came with it. Separate accounts keep the
history clean.

**Carry adequate liability coverage.** An umbrella policy sitting above your home
and auto limits is inexpensive relative to what it covers, and it is the first
line of defence — insurance that pays a claim means no one is looking at your
retirement accounts at all.

**Check your beneficiary designations.** They override your will. Assets passing
to a beneficiary directly can carry different protection than assets passing
through an estate.

**Consider how life insurance and annuities fit.** In states that protect them,
they can play a genuine role — though they should earn their place on their own
merits first, not solely for protection.

## The point

Creditor protection isn't about hiding anything. It's about not having your
retirement quietly exposed through a structure nobody ever examined. Most of the
useful moves are ordinary, inexpensive, and best made well before there's any
reason to need them.
